Where $179 billion in SBA small business loans went

An analysis of every funded SBA 7(a) loan from October 2019 to June 2026: which industries, products, lenders, and places get small business credit, what it costs, and how Virginia and the Richmond region compare.

Key findings
01

Lending grew through the rate spike instead of shrinking

The average initial rate on a 7(a) loan roughly doubled, from 5.42% in mid-2021 to a peak of 11.17% in early 2024, after the Federal Reserve began raising rates in March 2022. Lending still grew every year from FY2022 to FY2025.

FY2025 reached $32.1 billion, essentially tied with the FY2021 peak of $32.2 billion, but across 41% more loans (64,096 vs. 45,360). Borrowers kept coming, they just borrowed smaller amounts.

So what: higher rates coincided with smaller average loans, not fewer loans. Anyone sizing this market should track loan counts and loan sizes separately.

02

Most loans are small, but most dollars go to $1M+ loans

Loans under $150,000 make up 40% of all loans but only 4% of dollars. Loans of $1 million or more are 15% of loans and 63% of dollars.

The products split the same way. SBA Express is 39% of loans but under 8% of dollars, with a $104,770 average loan, a smaller SBA guarantee (52% vs. 79%), and a higher average rate (9.22% vs. 8.60% for Preferred Lender loans).

So what: "how much SBA lending happened" depends on whether you count loans or dollars. Counting loans highlights very small businesses, while counting dollars highlights large loans such as business acquisitions.

03

Restaurants and hotels get the largest share of dollars

Accommodation & Food Services received $31.4 billion (17.6%), the most of any industry, with an average loan of $739,360. Retail (13.8%) and Health Care (11.0%) follow, so the top three industries account for 42% of all dollars.

Construction has the most loans of any industry (43,592) but a much smaller average loan ($372,087), so it ranks fourth by dollars.

So what: SBA dollars are concentrated in a few capital-heavy industries, so a slowdown in restaurants, retail, or health care would show up quickly in the whole program.

04

Ten lenders control about a quarter of the market

About 2,150 lenders made at least one 7(a) loan, but the top 10 hold 27% of dollars and the top 50 hold 56%.

The leaders use very different strategies. Live Oak Banking Company leads in dollars (7.3%) with an average loan of $1.36 million, while Huntington makes the most loans (38,974) at an average of $258,429.

So what: the market is competitive but top-heavy, with one group of lenders specializing in large loans and another in high-volume small loans.

05

Newer businesses show more charge-offs among closed loans

Among loans with a disclosed outcome, 13.8% of loans to businesses two years old or younger were charged off, compared with 8.5% for established businesses and 4.5% for ownership changes.

Ownership-change loans are also the largest (average $1.13 million), the most often backed by collateral (97.7%), and carry the lowest average rate (7.88%).

So what: lenders appear to price acquisitions of existing businesses as the lowest-risk borrowers, and the outcomes so far are consistent with that.

Local view

Virginia and Greater Richmond

Virginia ranks 18th nationally by dollars, with $3.14 billion across 5,686 loans (1.8% of the national total). Its mix leans harder into two industries than the country does: restaurants and hotels take 22.1% of Virginia dollars (vs. 17.6% nationally), and professional and technical services take 12.6% (vs. 7.6%), which likely reflects the Northern Virginia services economy.

Method
  1. Source. SBA 7(a) FOIA loan data, FY2020 to present, as of June 30, 2026: 388,338 approved loans with 42 fields, downloaded from data.sba.gov (public domain).
  2. Cleaning in Python (pandas). Removed 50,104 cancelled loans that were approved but never funded, dropped borrower names and addresses, mapped 6-digit NAICS codes to 20 industry sectors, grouped 18 processing methods into 4 product families, and flagged the partial FY2026.
  3. Analysis in SQL. Loaded 338,234 funded loans into SQLite and answered 12 business questions with aggregate queries, CTEs, and window functions (RANK and ROW_NUMBER for lender concentration and state rankings).
  4. Excel. A workbook with SUMIFS-driven summaries, a state explorer with a dropdown, and every Virginia loan ready for PivotTables. It reconciles exactly to the SQL totals.
  5. Tableau. A 166,573-row aggregated extract feeds an interactive dashboard with a state map, filters, and trend charts.

Limitations
  1. No true default rate. SBA withholds the status of active loans under FOIA Exemption 4, so default risk can only be compared among closed loans.
  2. FY2026 is partial and starts late. The file has no FY2026 approvals before November 13, 2025. That gap lines up with the 43-day federal shutdown, which SBA said "halted approvals within SBA's flagship 7(a) and 504 programs."
  3. Approvals, not balances. Amounts are approved loan sizes, not what was drawn or is still owed.
  4. Jobs figures are self-reported. SBA notes that jobs created and retained are lender estimates it does not audit, so they are excluded from the findings.
  5. Averages are unweighted. Average rates treat every loan equally regardless of size.
